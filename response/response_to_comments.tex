\item[R1-1.] \origcomment{Authors should provide PRISMA workflow describing data collection
process.}
\filledresponse{We thank the reviewer for this comment. HR-VILAGE-3K3M's collection builds directly on the PRISMA-style search-and-screen
protocol already published in Lan et al. (F1000Research 2025, 14:493) (their Figure 1: database/Google
searches $\rightarrow$ duplicate removal $\rightarrow$ full-text screening $\rightarrow$ 55 included
datasets), rather than an independent search process, so we report our collection as an explicit
extension of that workflow instead of duplicating their diagram. Of Lan et al.'s 55 datasets: 32 are
carried over directly, and 7 more are carried over at finer granularity (their SuperSeries
accessions correspond to SubSeries we cite individually, e.g.\ their GSE74817 $\rightarrow$ our
GSE74811/74813/74815/74816). Fifteen are not included, each for a specific reason: 3 are duplicate
cohorts superseded by GSE73072 (already in our collection), 2 are non-gene-expression assays
(CITE-seq/scTCR-seq), 1 is controlled-access, 1 is an in~vitro (not in~vivo response) study, 1 used
platelets rather than whole blood/PBMC, and 7 ImmPort-only accessions had a data-quality issue or no
deposited expression data. HR-VILAGE-3K3M then extends this base with 19 additional studies
identified via the same search protocol applied to more recent records: 7 were published after Lan
et al.'s search (2024--2025), and, notably, 4 (GSE201533, GSE190747, GSE199750, GSE201642) predate
Lan et al.'s search but were not captured by it, indicating our search protocol has complementary
coverage rather than simply being run later in time. The remaining 8 additions are 2 studies in a
``mixed exposure'' category that does not exist in Lan et al.'s inoculation/vaccination framework,
and 5 additional single-cell RNA-seq studies. The complete per-study accounting (accession, category,
and inclusion/exclusion status) is provided in Supplementary Table 1.}

\filledresponse{We thank the reviewer for this comment. We computed subject-level age coverage and the $\geq$60y count for each platform.
Microarray: 1,779 unique subjects, of whom 1,166 have an exact recorded age (423, 36\%, are

\item[R1-4.] \origcomment{Define Inoculation and mixed exposure.}
\filledresponse{We thank the reviewer for this comment. \textbf{Inoculation} refers to controlled human infection model (human challenge)
studies, in which healthy volunteers are deliberately exposed to a defined dose of a respiratory
virus under controlled clinical conditions, allowing precise control over exposure timing and dose
relative to sampling -- as distinct from natural, community-acquired infection. \textbf{Mixed
exposure} refers to studies in which subjects experience more than one type of immune exposure,
in combination or in sequence: post-vaccination infection (breakthrough infection after
vaccination), post-infection vaccination (vaccination in previously infected/seropositive
individuals), and natural infection without a controlled inoculation. These definitions will be
added explicitly to the manuscript (Background and Summary or Methods) rather than left implicit in
the summary statistics, as currently. \textbf{[Jing, please check this definition and confirm/refine
the definition of ``mixed exposure,'' since that term is specific to our own categorization rather
than a standard field term.]}}

\item[R1-5.] \origcomment{How are single cell data dealt? How are cell types defined -- clarify on
those details.}
\filledresponse{We thank the reviewer for this comment. Processing (already in Methods, \texttt{template.tex} lines 258 and 275): source
files (h5ad, 10x matrix, or R objects, with or without metadata) are integrated into a Python
AnnData object per study, with cell barcodes prefixed by dataset identifier to ensure global
uniqueness; the primary expression matrix is verified/reassigned from raw count layers; genes
expressed in fewer than 10 cells are removed; total-count normalization (target sum 10,000) and
\texttt{log1p} are applied; and metadata fields are standardized across datasets (missing columns
added as NA) for consistent downstream integration. Cell type definition: most constituent
scRNA-seq studies already include author-provided cell-type annotations in their deposited
metadata, which we retain as-is. Where a study lacks curated annotations (e.g., GSE201533/GSE201534,
used in the Cell Type Annotation validation), we apply \texttt{SingleR} (reference-based) and
\texttt{GPTCelltype} (LLM-based) and reconcile the two, as described in Technical Validation.
\textbf{[Yiran, please confirm whether this reconciliation approach was applied to every study
lacking curated annotations, or only to the GSE201533/GSE201534 pair shown as the validation
example, so the response accurately reflects what was done resource-wide.]}}

\item[R1-6.] \origcomment{Donors with high baseline titers mask seroconversion rates. How are
these donors dealt with? Authors should consider using baseline adjusted methods for response
categorization (maxRBA from Steve Kleinstein's group).}
\filledresponse{We thank the reviewer for this comment. Response status for influenza vaccine studies is defined by Maximum Fold Change
(MFC) in HAI titer, following the HIPC-CHI Signatures Project/HIPC-I Consortium consensus definition (\textit{Sci.
Immunol.} 2017): high
responders have MFC~$\geq$4 and Day~28 HAI~$\geq$40, non-responders MFC~$\leq$1, and the remainder
are moderate responders (\texttt{template.tex}, line 279). We agree this MFC-based definition does
not itself adjust for baseline titer, so high-baseline-titer donors with limited room to rise can be
undercounted as non-responders. Rather than impose a single baseline-adjusted definition (e.g.\
maxRBA) across all studies, we provide the underlying raw baseline and post-vaccination HAI titers
for every subject in the \texttt{antibody} folder, precisely so users can compute maxRBA or any
other baseline-adjusted metric themselves for their own analysis, alongside our default MFC-based
\texttt{responder} field. This will be stated explicitly in the manuscript alongside the existing
responder-definition text.}

\item[R1-7.] \origcomment{How are they defining response status for other vaccines?}
\filledresponse{We thank the reviewer for this comment. Unlike influenza, where HAI titer is measured uniformly across nearly all
constituent studies (enabling the single MFC-based definition in R1-6), COVID-19 vaccine studies in
HR-VILAGE-3K3M measure antibody response using different assays from study to study (binding vs.\
neutralizing antibody, against different antigens/variants), so we did not find a way to apply one
uniform response definition across them without discarding information or forcing an inappropriate
common assay. Instead: where the source study itself defined a responder/outcome classification, we
retain that study-specific definition in the metadata; where it did not, we provide the raw binding
and/or neutralizing antibody measurements in the \texttt{antibody} folder so users can define their
own response criteria appropriate to the assay and research question, consistent with the existing
statement in Methods (\texttt{template.tex}, line 279: ``For COVID-19 vaccine studies, where no
universal response definition exists, we provided binding and neutralizing antibody data in the
antibody folder, allowing users to define customized outcome criteria...''). We will expand this
sentence to explicitly state the reason (heterogeneous assays across studies, unlike HAI for
influenza) rather than leaving it implicit.}

\item[R1-8.] \origcomment{Authors should validate the overall processing data by checking known
vaccination read outs -- interferon signature at day 1, plasmablast expansion at day 7 for
influenza vaccine. Authors should consider checking these signatures at these time points in case
of transcriptome cell types.}
\filledresponse{We thank the reviewer for this comment. Done. New Technical Validation subsection, ``Known Vaccination Response
Signatures'' (\texttt{template.tex}, after Batch-Effect Correction Assessment): using paired,
within-subject module scores (baseline-normalized) computed from literature-standard marker genes
across microarray and RNA-seq influenza vaccine studies, the interferon-stimulated-gene signature
increases from baseline to Day 1 in 86.9\% of 412 paired subjects (Wilcoxon $p=2.9\times10^{-56}$),
and the plasmablast signature increases from baseline to Day 7 in 81.3\% of 713 paired subjects
($p=5.0\times10^{-67}$) -- both consistent with the expected biology. We note RNA-seq contributes
only 5 paired subjects per comparison, so this check is presently powered mainly by the
microarray-derived subset; a per-platform breakdown is stated in the text. We have not yet extended
this check to cell-type-resolved expression within the scRNA-seq data specifically; that is a
natural follow-up if the reviewer considers it necessary for this revision.}

\item[R2-1.] \origcomment{(compare with ImmPort) Explicit positioning of this resource amidst
related resources like ImmPort or HIPC (and associated visualizer ImmuneSpace) should be
mentioned in Introduction. Without this explicit positioning, a reader would find it hard to
assess the extra value added by this resource atop existing resources like ImmPort. For instance,
ImmPort had compiled 300 studies based on its 2018 publication in Scientific Data. The current
work takes 66 studies, probably many of them from ImmPort, relevant to respiratory viral
immunization (including both influenza and COVID) and harmonizes them. Within this context,
answering the questions below directly in the paper can clarify the value addition of the
HR-VILAGE resource: (i) What fraction of studies in HR-VILAGE are taken from ImmPort? (ii) Of the
studies taken from ImmPort, which harmonization or data cleaning was missing in ImmPort, and what
curation/analysis has been additionally done in HR-VILAGE-3K3M to address these harmonization
issues? For instance, it is hard to believe that ImmPort did not already handle gene ID issues
like SEPTIN14. (iii) For the studies in HR-VILAGE taken from GEO and ArrayExpress, was more effort
required to perform data cleaning and harmonization (compared to studies taken from ImmPort)?}
\filledresponse{We thank the reviewer for this comment. (i) 3/66 studies (4.5\%; SDY311, SDY112,
SDY315, all one underlying cohort) have ImmPort as their expression-data source; a further 10 use
ImmPort only for antibody/response data (13/66, 20\%, touch ImmPort in any capacity). (ii)--(iii)
All three ImmPort studies required the same processing as raw GEO microarray data: ImmPort
deposited only raw Illumina BeadArray signal (SDY311) or raw Affymetrix CEL files (SDY112,
SDY315) -- none pre-normalized or gene-symbol-standardized. We confirmed this directly for SDY311:
its raw file is not quantile-normalized (per-sample signal distributions differ substantially) and
uses outdated gene symbols (e.g.\ \texttt{SEPT14}, \texttt{SEPT12}, \texttt{SEPP1} -- the pre-2017
nomenclature HGNC replaced due to Excel date-corruption) rather than current HGNC symbols, i.e.\
precisely the SEPTIN14-type issue raised, still present and unresolved in ImmPort's own file. We
applied the same pipeline used for GEO microarray studies (Methods, line 255): probe collapsing (up
to 17 probes per gene), \texttt{HGNChelper} symbol standardization, $\log_2$, and QN, then merged in
subject-level HAI titers from ImmPort's separate schema under our standardized responder
definition. ImmPort-sourced data therefore required comparable harmonization effort to GEO-sourced
data. This positioning has been added to Background and Summary.}

\item[R2-2a.] \origcomment{(other modalities) A key limitation of HR-VILAGE-3K3M compared to
ImmPort is its restriction to transcriptomic/antibody-response data, versus ImmPort's additional
support for flow cytometry, cytokine, CyTOF, and related datasets. What is the rationale for this
restriction, given that systems immunology research has largely advanced in recent times due to
integrative analysis of multi-modal data collected from immune-related human cohorts? Won't this
restriction limit the utility of HR-VILAGE for future systems immunology analyses or
re-analyses? This limitation either needs to be acknowledged, alongside the advantages of
HR-VILAGE over ImmPort; or addressed by including analysis-ready data pertaining to some of these
extra modalities in HR-VILAGE.}
\filledresponse{We thank the reviewer for this comment. Gene expression (plus antibody response)
is HR-VILAGE-3K3M's core, deliberate scope, harmonized uniformly across all 66 studies -- this is
what we can offer consistently. Most constituent studies simply have no other modality available
at all, so broadening scope is not a matter of choice for them. A minority do: from our curation
records, 13/66 studies have companion data not included in HR-VILAGE -- GSE102012, GSE48023/GSE48018,
GSE250023, GSE207750, and GSE194378 have companion multi-omic data at their GEO/Synapse source
(scATAC-seq, scRNA-seq, CITE-seq, autoantibody panels, DNA genotyping); and 8 of our 13
ImmPort-linked studies (whose antibody data traces to ImmPort SDY accessions) additionally have
flow cytometry, MBAA (multiplex bead array assay), CyTOF, mass spectrometry, or Q-PCR data within
those ImmPort records (the remaining 5 ImmPort-linked studies have no companion modality). All are
now listed with accessions in new Supplementary Table S2. Including these is out of scope for the
present Data Descriptor (see E1);
we cite them so interested users can pursue them directly. We acknowledge this scope limitation
explicitly in Usage Notes.}

\item[R2-2b.] \origcomment{A related point on missing modalities is different assays of
immune/antibody response such as MN (microneutralization) titers, HAI, and ELISPOT. There is
mention of HAI in the paper, but the paper is not clear about whether other assays such as MN and
ELISPOT are stored in HR-VILAGE. Are there studies that have jointly assayed these different
immune endpoints? If so, it would be useful to store these different endpoints in HR-VILAGE to
enable alternative definitions of antibody-responders (continuous-valued as well as binary) to
compare/contrast these outcome definitions from biological and statistical viewpoints.}
\filledresponse{We thank the reviewer for this comment. We checked the actual stored columns
across the 36 studies with antibody data. Influenza studies (22/36) uniformly use HAI titers, as
already stated -- including all 13 studies that draw on ImmPort in any capacity (3 ImmPort-primary,
10 with GEO expression/ImmPort antibody), which we confirmed store strain-specific HAI titers by
inspecting each file's columns directly. COVID-19 studies (14/36) are more heterogeneous: most store binding IgG (e.g.\
spike/RBD/nucleocapsid, across variants), and several jointly store additional endpoint types --
e.g.\ GSE246525 has binding IgG, a surrogate virus neutralization titer (sVNT, an MN-equivalent),
and an IGRA (interferon-gamma release, T-cell) readout together; GSE201533 has binding IgG plus an
ACE2-competition (neutralization-inhibition) assay. We did not find literal ELISPOT data in the
studies checked. So joint multi-endpoint data does exist for several studies and is already
included as-is (raw values, one column per endpoint) in the \texttt{antibody} folder; we have not
imposed a single derived outcome across endpoint types, precisely so users can define
continuous-valued or alternative binary outcomes themselves, consistent with our response to R1-6.}

\item[R2-3.] \origcomment{(compare with other repo) Comparative analysis of this resource with
other independent state-of-the-art resources is missing. While all studies in HR-VILAGE may not
be available in other independent resources, certain studies may also be present in other
transcriptomic resources such as recount2/3 and Gene Expression Commons (GEXC). For such common
studies, what is the qualitative difference between the data preprocessing pipelines employed by
the different resources, and can the authors quantify the similarities and differences in these
resources' processed datasets and downstream results?}
\filledresponse{We thank the reviewer for this comment. We checked systematically against
recount3, which provides a machine-readable project index: of our 66 studies, 3 RNA-seq studies
(GSE45735, GSE117580, GSE207750; identified by resolving each study's SRA accession and checking
recount3's index) are covered. For all 3, we compared recount3's independently re-aligned
gene-level counts (Gencode v26, their own pipeline) against our processed values for every
matched sample (421 samples total, matched via GEO sample titles, using $\sim$35,000--40,500
common genes per study): Spearman correlation is consistently high -- GSE45735: 54/54 samples,
mean $r=0.969$ (SD 0.001); GSE207750: 271/271 samples, mean $r=0.984$ (SD 0.010); GSE117580:
96/96 samples, mean $r=0.950$ (SD 0.001). The qualitative difference is that recount3 uniformly
re-aligns raw reads with its own Monorail pipeline against a fixed annotation, whereas HR-VILAGE-3K3M
generally uses each study's own processed/count data where available (Methods, line 255); despite
this pipeline difference, the two resources agree closely at the gene-expression level for every
overlapping study we could check. We did not find a comparable machine-readable project index for
GEXC to run the same systematic check; GEXC's scope (sorted immune/stem-cell reference populations)
makes study-level overlap with our vaccine/inoculation cohorts unlikely, but we have not
exhaustively verified this.}

\item[R2-4a.] \origcomment{(why QN, explain AUC) What was the rationale for selecting quantile
normalization (QN) over alternative approaches for RNAseq harmonization? Was QN performed in a
class-specific manner (i.e., stratified by responder group as high, medium, low) rather than
across the pooled dataset? Prior work has shown that whole-dataset QN can distort true biological
differences, particularly when class-effect proportions are high, whereas class-specific QN
better preserves such differences (Zhao, Wong \& Goh, Sci Rep, 2020:
\url{https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-72664-6}).}
\responsespace

\item[R2-4b.] \origcomment{On a related point, could the authors clarify whether the Transformer
model used for antibody responder prediction was trained directly on QN-normalized gene
expression values? If so, how much of the Transformer's predictive performance (AUC = 0.737) can
be attributed to genuine biological signal vs. artifacts of the QN-induced rank-based rescaling?}
\responsespace

\item[R2-5a.] \origcomment{(study specific pipeline) While standardized/uniform (pre)processing
pipelines employed by HR-VILAGE is preferable to reduce methodological variations, another viable
strategy is to preprocess datasets from different studies in a highly study-specific manner to
account for study-specific artifacts. Given this context, it is highly advisable for HR-VILAGE to
not only provide the standardized analysis-ready data for a study, but also the analysis-ready
data provided by the study's authors whenever available (e.g., from websites/GitHub linked to
papers such as illustrated here: \url{https://www.tsanglab.org/resources}).}
\responsespace

\item[R2-5b.] \origcomment{Conversely, focusing on RNAseq data, gene counts data provided by study
authors is used for certain studies in HR-VILAGE, and gene counts from standardized analysis
(alignment of fastq files and counts summarization) is used for other studies. For each study,
both study-author-provided and uniformly-processed pipeline based versions of the gene counts
matrix should be provided -- i.e., even when RNAseq-based gene counts are available for certain
studies, HR-VILAGE should also provide gene counts recomputed from its standard pipeline. The

useful for QC comparison (e.g., tSNE/PCA) to flag samples with discrepancies between the two
pipelines.}
\responsespace

\item[R2-6.] \origcomment{(compare application results with publications) For any of the three
applications shown, please compare your results with previously published results to indicate
what is common, and more importantly, what is different, so that there is motivation to do
